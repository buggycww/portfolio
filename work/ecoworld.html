EcoWorld / Meta Quest 3 Project capture

EcoWorld was my final-year project: an MR serious game about ecological literacy. The goal was not to teach a list of facts, but to let players feel how a small change propagates through an ecosystem.

Mixed reality HFSM AI Simulation Serious game Solo build

The problem

Ecological systems are full of delayed consequences. A population rises, a resource depletes, the weather shifts, and the effect arrives later. Traditional diagrams explain that chain; they do not let you feel it.

MR gave me a different canvas: a living ecosystem on a table, at human scale, where the player can lean in and change a variable. The design question became: how much of the simulation should the player see, and how quickly should a consequence read?

The system

EcoWorld is built from three interlocking layers. Each one can change the others, which is what makes the ecosystem feel alive rather than scripted.

01 / Organisms

Behaviour

A hierarchical FSM drives organism behaviour and needs. Organisms respond to environmental pressure without one monolithic update loop.

02 / Environment

Conditions

Time-of-day and weather change the conditions organisms live in, so the same species can behave differently as the world shifts.

03 / Stressors

Pressure

Disaster events introduce dynamic stressors such as overpopulation and disease, pushing the system into states the player has to respond to.

The hard part wasn’t simulating the ecosystem. It was making the simulation legible.

What I owned

I owned the design and code end to end: the simulation architecture, the HFSM organism behaviour, the environmental and event systems, the interaction feedback, and the build on Quest 3. I also ran the project through playtests and iterated on what players actually noticed.

  • Simulation architecture: separated organisms, environment, and event pressure so each could be tuned independently.
  • Readable feedback: prioritised states and consequences the player could perceive in the headset over simulation detail they could not.
  • Device constraints: kept the experience honest on standalone hardware rather than assuming a desktop budget.

The hard part

A simulation with many interacting variables can easily become noise. I had to make choices about what the player sees, what the system hides, and how long a consequence is allowed to take before it stops reading as cause and effect.

That is a design problem as much as a programming one. The answer was not “simulate more.” It was to make the important variables visible, keep the loop short enough to learn from, and let the player form a hypothesis before the system confirms it.

Outcome

A national result

EcoWorld was awarded Second Runner-Up at the Malaysia Creative Tech Competition 2026, selected from 87 entries across 23 institutions.

More useful than the placement: the project proved I can take an ambiguous brief, design a system around it, and ship a coherent experience on a constrained device.

What I’d do next

Stronger onboarding: teach the causal loop before asking the player to optimise it. A more explicit performance budget per scenario. And a content pipeline that lets new ecological scenarios be authored without touching the simulation core.
